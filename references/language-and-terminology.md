Apply this reference whenever producing manuscript-ready English for a medical
review section or submission component.

## Reconstruction And Drafting Integrity

For translation reconstruction, preserve every authorized fact, clinical
stratification, causal or conditional relationship, comparison, and evidence
strength. Preserve the author reasoning skeleton and functional order. Rebuild
English syntax freely; do not rebuild medical meaning. Put substantive
information reordering into one consolidated author decision.

For direct drafting, synthesize the authorized propositions in the approved
functional order. Without an approved order, organize by argument rather than
by any one source's sentence order. Do not invent a fact, causal link, hierarchy,
outcome, or recommendation. Draft supportable content and flag a precise
verification need when only a limited point remains unresolved.

## From Design To Medical English

All English drafting and revision, including direct drafting from a plan, uses
the language rules below. An approved plan supplies semantic and organizational
constraints, not finished sentences. Build complete syntax, clear subjects and
referents, and coherent sentence relations; do not turn each analysis bullet
into a sentence mechanically. Preserve the approved argument and paragraph job
while realizing them in natural medical prose. Content selection and treatment
of editor instructions are owned by `structure-and-prose.md`.

When original prose or an author-approved example is available, distinguish
its protected content from its useful organizational and expression patterns.
Preserve effective author choices rather than normalizing them merely for
convention. Transfer only approved form from an expression reference, never
unrelated medical facts, populations, or recommendations. If no reference is
provided, use the language defaults here without inventing a personal style or
requiring an example before safe drafting.

## Sentence Architecture

- Put clarity before sentence variety and meaning before source-language word
  order.
- Prefer explicit subjects, precise predicates, and visible logical relations.
- Split clauses when nested conditions, contrasts, or relative clauses increase
  cognitive load. Vary sentence form only when clarity is preserved; avoid both
  repetitive short-sentence sequences and decorative complexity.
- Use active voice when the actor matters. Use passive voice when the process,
  evidence, or clinical object is the legitimate information focus.
- Form transitions through causal, contrastive, narrowing, or topic continuity;
  do not rely on mechanical sequences such as `first`, `second`, and `moreover`.
- Use continuous prose for manuscript narration unless the component contract,
  journal, or user requires a list.
- Do not use the em dash character `—` in generated or revised manuscript text.
  Recast the relation with punctuation or syntax without adding complexity.
  Preserve verbatim quoted text and official names unless alteration is
  authorized.

## Lexical Choice

Prioritize semantic precision, then familiar usage, then rhetorical variety.
Use common academic words for general reasoning and domain-specific terminology
only when it carries a necessary medical distinction. Prefer specific verbs and
concrete clinical or evidentiary nouns to vague verbs, nominalizations,
decorative adjectives, intensifiers, or promotional language. Do not vary a
technical term merely to avoid repetition, and do not substitute rare words to
make prose sound more advanced.

Avoid generic framing or self-praise when it replaces a specific contribution,
clinical meaning, or evidence boundary. Match verb strength to evidence; a
stronger verb is not automatically better writing.

Do not use an avoid/use-instead list as an unqualified replacement table.
Before substituting a word or compressing a phrase, compare the proposition,
actor, scientific action, causality, quantity, evidence strength, terminology,
genre, and sentence function. Do not prefer a shorter or more ordinary word when
it changes any of those properties.

## Terminology Control

- Use one primary term for one concept unless the authorized terminology
  contract distinguishes variants.
- Preserve confirmed boundaries among anatomy, mechanism, phenotype, clinical
  scenario, treatment objective, procedure, and evidence status.
- Use an established specialist term when it is more exact than a general
  expression, not because it sounds more sophisticated.
- Treat claims that a term is standard, preferred, or guideline-endorsed as
  evidence-dependent. Do not hard-code disease-specific terminology in this
  general editing method.

## Abbreviations And Language Variant

- Treat the abstract and main text as separate abbreviation domains. Treat each
  figure and table as a stand-alone reading unit under the journal's rules.
- Define an abbreviation at first use within its required domain, then use it
  consistently. In main-text headings, retain an already defined abbreviation
  unless the heading would be ambiguous out of context or the journal requires
  the full term.
- Do not add `s` when an abbreviation functions as a modifier or established
  collective concept. Use a grammatical plural when it denotes genuinely
  countable multiple entities and domain convention permits it. Do not infer
  plurality mechanically from the final letter of an abbreviation.
- When a target journal is unknown, preserve the English variant already used
  in the draft. For drafting from Chinese with no English baseline, use American
  English. Apply the target journal's language convention once verified.

## Citation-Anchor Integrity

Preserve citation identities and their logical attachments during direct
drafting, translation, or revision. Use only citation identities available in
the authorized materials. After splitting or merging sentences, place each
citation next to the claim it actually supports; do not shift a citation onto a
new or broader claim. Do not delete, invent, add, or reassign citations unless
the user authorizes citation review. Flag ambiguous original anchors. Apply the
target journal's citation and punctuation style rather than a presumed
universal British or American rule.
