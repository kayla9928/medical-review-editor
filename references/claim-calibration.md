## Goal

Make claims as strong as the evidence permits and as limited as accuracy
requires. The goal is calibrated confidence, not automatic caution.

## Balance Test

For each claim, identify:

- claim type: descriptive, mechanistic, classificatory, diagnostic,
  prognostic, therapeutic, guideline, implementation, or research-gap;
- claim basis: verified evidence/guideline fact, author synthesis or
  interpretation, author organizational framework, or unverified outcome,
  extrapolation, comparison, forecast, or practice advocacy;
- population and setting;
- intervention, comparator, or target when relevant;
- endpoint or outcome;
- uncertainty source.

Then choose the strength supported by the authorized evidence and the latest
confirmed author decision:

| Claim basis or evidence situation | Preferred posture |
|---|---|
| Guideline or high-quality evidence clearly supports the point | state directly |
| Evidence supports direction but not magnitude or hierarchy | state direction and scope |
| Evidence is mixed or population-limited | name the condition or limitation once |
| Mechanistic rationale suggests a role but clinical evidence is limited | frame as rationale, hypothesis, or selected use |
| Author synthesis or interpretation | state the supported integrative contribution directly and distinguish it from source fact when that distinction matters |
| Author organizational framework | describe its organizing function directly; use `proposed` only when that is its intended status, not as a reflex |
| Unverified outcome, comparative effect, extrapolation, forecast, or practice advocacy | retain a specific limitation or verification question that affects interpretation or use |

Do not turn an author framework into a validated algorithm, universal guideline,
deterministic clinical pathway, or fixed procedure-to-scenario mapping unless
the authorized evidence source establishes that status. Conversely, do not
replace a supported organizational or explanatory contribution with empty
disclaimers merely because it is author-created.

Without evidence verification, do not weaken or strengthen a claim merely
because an editor prefers a narrower, stronger, shorter, or more conventional
form. Preserve the confirmed strength and flag the exact verification need.

## Scientific-Action Comparison

Compare the source and candidate predicates before editing. Preserve distinctions
among:

- `detect`, `measure`, `quantify`, `assay`, `assess`, `evaluate`, and
  `determine`;
- `show`, `demonstrate`, `indicate`, `suggest`, and `support`;
- `cause`, `drive`, `contribute to`, and `associate with`; and
- `recommend`, `prefer`, `consider`, and `permit`.

Treat a change within any set as a possible change in action, evidence, agency,
or recommendation strength, not as stylistic substitution.

## Contribution Status And Terminology

When an authorized revision changes contribution status, distinguish the claim
being withdrawn from the explanatory or organizational work that remains.
Changing a label does not establish novelty, and reducing a novelty claim does
not require erasing useful synthesis. Judge terms such as framework or pathway
by their actual asserted function and evidentiary status, not by a word ban.
Do not replace a challenged classification with a renamed equivalent.

## Anti-Defensive Adaptation

`structure-and-prose.md` owns the separation of editing constraints from prose
content. Here, calibrate boundaries that actually affect scientific meaning or
reader interpretation; do not add a manuscript disclaimer merely to echo an
instruction given to the editor.

Borrow the useful principle from anti-defensive writing: lead with what the text
does, shows, explains, or contributes. Do not begin important paragraphs by
apologizing for what the manuscript does not prove.

Keep a limitation only when it improves accuracy, safety, method transparency,
or clinical interpretation. Place it where it belongs: often in evidence
synthesis, discussion, limitations, or implementation boundaries, not
reflexively in every claim sentence.

For author frameworks, lead with purpose and operational contribution. Preserve
necessary boundaries through positive definition, complementarity,
applicability conditions, predominant-pattern language, and heterogeneity when
those forms carry the same limit. Avoid reflexive `not ... but ...`, `not
intended to ... Rather ...`, or similar disclaimer-first templates when a
direct formulation remains accurate. Use an explicit negative construction
when omitting it would create a material scientific category error.

## Rewrite Pattern

1. State the useful claim.
2. Attach the correct scope.
3. Name the evidence basis when needed.
4. Add a limitation only if it changes interpretation or use.

Avoid:

- repeated "does not claim", "cannot prove", "may potentially", "to be clear";
- treating every author framework as a disclaimer, a guideline, or a validated
  algorithm;
- adding caveats that protect against hypothetical objections but do not improve
  clinical accuracy;
- using stock appraisals such as `highly promising`, `holds great potential`,
  `may revolutionize`, or `warrants further randomized trials` in place of the
  observed result, evidence design, unresolved outcome, or feasible next study.

## Medical Safety Boundary

Do not remove limitations that affect patient population, contraindications,
adverse events, recommendation strength, procedural expertise, feasibility, or
external validity. In medical writing, confident prose must still preserve
clinical boundaries.
